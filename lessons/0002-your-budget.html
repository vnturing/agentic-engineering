Your budget: attention and skill

Lesson 1: an agent’s attention is finite, so you manage its context. Now the mirror image: your own attention and skill are finite too, and the way you use the agent decides whether they grow or shrink.

1. The finding

In Anthropic’s randomized trial, 52 mostly junior engineers learned an unfamiliar Python library (Trio), with or without an AI assistant, then took a quiz on what they had just used. The AI group averaged 50% against 67% for the hand-coders. The AI group was only about two minutes faster, and that speed difference wasn’t statistically significant.

The largest gap was on debugging questions. That matters for our mission: debugging and code reading are the skills you use to review what an agent wrote. The lesson-1 lever “verify” only works if a human can still judge the result.

2. It depends on how you use it

The researchers grouped participants by interaction style. The dividing line was who did the thinking.

flowchart TB
  S["You + AI, unfamiliar code"] --> W{"Who does the thinking?"}
  W -->|"AI does"| L["Low: 24 to 39% quiz"]
  W -->|"You do"| H["High: 65 to 86% quiz"]
  L --> L1["Delegation 39%"]
  L --> L2["Progressive reliance 35%"]
  L --> L3["Iterative AI debugging 24%"]
  H --> H1["Generation then comprehension 86%"]
  H --> H2["Hybrid code and explanation 68%"]
  H --> H3["Conceptual inquiry 65%"]

Two details to notice. First, generation-then-comprehension looked nearly the same as pure delegation except that these people asked follow-up questions to check their own understanding afterwards. Second, conceptual inquiry (asking only conceptual questions, then writing the code yourself) was the fastest high-scoring pattern and second fastest overall. Understanding wasn’t a tax on speed here. Note that the “iterative AI debugging” group used AI to fix errors rather than to understand them, and finished slowest with the lowest score.

3. How far to trust this

Small: 52 people, and each interaction pattern had only 2 to 7 of them.
Associative: the authors say the patterns are not shown to cause the scores.
Short horizon: a quiz right after the task, on one library, by the vendor of an AI product.
Different from agents: this was a chat sidebar. The authors expect agentic tools like Claude Code to have effects that are likely more pronounced.
Learning vs. doing: Anthropic’s separate study found large speedups on tasks where people already had the skills. The two can both be true.

A working rule (my inference, not the study’s): in familiar territory, delegate freely and verify. In unfamiliar territory, keep yourself in the loop: ask for explanations, predict before you read, and write the core of the thing yourself. Getting stuck on purpose is part of the cost of learning, as the authors suggest.
